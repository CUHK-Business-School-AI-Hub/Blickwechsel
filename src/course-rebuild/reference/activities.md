# A menu of classroom activities

Twenty-eight formats in eight families. Offer the lecturer **two or three that
fit the finding**, with a one-line trade-off each, and let them choose. Never
pick silently.

The family names say what the student does, not what the technique is called.

Each entry gives: what happens, what it is good for, roughly how long, and the
thing that goes wrong.

---

## 1. Answer before you are told

Everyone commits before anyone hears the answer. The cheapest improvement
available to most lectures, because the questions are usually already there.

**Four options, one right**
Students pick from four; every wrong option is a named misunderstanding.
*Good for:* a definition or rule that students think they already know.
*Time:* 5–8 min. *Watch out for:* a wrong option nobody would pick — worse than
having none.

**Predict, then run it**
Students write down what the data or demonstration will show, then you run it.
*Good for:* anything with a counter-intuitive result.
*Time:* 6–10 min. *Watch out for:* predictions must be written, not thought.

**Answer, argue, answer again**
Answer alone, argue in pairs, answer again. Show both distributions.
*Good for:* topics where the wrong answer is popular and confidently held.
*Time:* 10–15 min. *Watch out for:* needs a genuinely split question, or the
second vote is identical and the point is lost.

**How sure are you?**
Answer plus a confidence rating; credit for being right *and* calibrated.
*Good for:* teaching students that confident and correct are different things.
*Time:* 6–10 min. *Watch out for:* explain the scoring or it feels like a trick.

## 2. Break it, then understand it

The student meets the failure before the rule. What they discover themselves
does not need to be remembered.

**Break your own answer**
They compute something, then one new observation arrives and they redo it.
*Good for:* any method with a known weakness — small samples, outliers, extrapolation.
*Time:* 10–15 min. *Watch out for:* the second result must move visibly. If the
change is small the lesson does not land.

**Find the planted error**
A complete, confident worked answer with exactly one thing wrong.
*Good for:* procedures students will have to check in others' work.
*Time:* 10–20 min. *Watch out for:* say how many errors there are, or they hunt forever.

**Worked, half-worked, blank**
Three problems: the first fully worked, the second half, the third empty.
*Good for:* mechanical procedures, and for weak or anxious groups.
*Time:* 15–25 min. *Watch out for:* fading too fast; the middle step is the one
that teaches.

**The deliberate wrong turn**
You work it wrongly at the front, on purpose, and keep going until someone stops you.
*Good for:* one specific error you know they make every year.
*Time:* 5 min. *Watch out for:* commit fully, and never let it stand uncorrected.

## 3. Judge someone else's work

No production at all. The student decides whether finished work is any good —
which is what most graduates actually do.

**Would you sign this?**
A confident, professional analysis with a real flaw. Sign or refuse, and say why.
*Good for:* the skill that survives the tools changing. **Use the
`case-authoring` skill to design it** — one concept, arithmetic right and
conclusion wrong, and not every claim problematic.
*Time:* 20–30 min. *Watch out for:* if every claim is wrong, blanket suspicion
scores full marks.

**Two answers, same data**
Two analyses reaching opposite conclusions from identical numbers. Which do you believe?
*Good for:* showing that method choice is a judgement, not a lookup.
*Time:* 15–20 min. *Watch out for:* both must be defensible, or it is a spot-the-error.

**Rank these four**
Four submitted answers, best to worst, with the ordering justified.
*Good for:* teaching a marking scheme from the inside. Excellent before an assessment.
*Time:* 15–20 min. *Watch out for:* the middle two should be genuinely arguable.

**Write the referee report**
Review a piece of work against a rubric and recommend accept, revise or reject.
*Good for:* senior students, and anything with a professional review analogue.
*Time:* 25–40 min, often homework. *Watch out for:* give them the rubric first.

## 4. Make the call

A decision with numbers attached and no single obvious answer. The closest
thing to the job.

**Which option, and what would change your mind**
Two or three priced alternatives, a stated expectation, pick one.
*Good for:* deriving a concept from a decision rather than defining it first.
*Time:* 12–18 min. *Watch out for:* the second half of the question — the number
that flips the decision — is where the learning is. Do not drop it for time.

**Which assumption matters most**
Given a model, find the assumption that most changes the answer.
*Good for:* teaching that not all uncertainty is equal.
*Time:* 10–15 min. *Watch out for:* needs a model they can actually vary.

**Choose the method, and defend it**
Several defensible approaches, choose and justify against stated criteria.
*Good for:* the point in a course where students have several tools and no rule
for choosing.
*Time:* 12–20 min. *Watch out for:* the criteria have to be given, or it becomes
a preference poll.

## 5. Say it briefly, in your own words

Short, bounded writing. The constraint is the teaching: a page hides confusion,
five sentences do not.

**One-minute paper**
What was the main point, and what is still unclear? Collected on the way out.
*Good for:* finding out what the room actually took away. Almost free.
*Time:* 3–5 min. *Watch out for:* only worth it if you read them and respond next time.

**Write the email**
Turn the analysis into five sentences for someone who will not read the workings.
*Good for:* everything students will do in practice. Ruthlessly exposes fake understanding.
*Time:* 10–15 min. *Watch out for:* enforce the length limit.

**Recommendation in paragraph one**
A short memo where the answer must come first and the reasoning after.
*Good for:* professional writing habits, and for revealing whether they have an
answer at all.
*Time:* 20–30 min. *Watch out for:* mark the structure, not the prose style.

**Draw it before any formula**
Sketch the relationship you expect, then check it against the data.
*Good for:* quantitative topics where students reach for arithmetic too early.
*Time:* 5–10 min. *Watch out for:* no axes labels means no thinking; require them.

## 6. Argue it out with each other

Structures that wrap around any other format. They change who does the talking,
not what is taught.

**Think, pair, share**
Alone, then in pairs, then to the room.
*Good for:* almost anything. The default upgrade for a question that currently
gets silence.
*Time:* adds 4–6 min. *Watch out for:* give a time limit for each phase or the
first phase eats everything.

**Each group learns one piece, then teaches it**
Groups become the expert on one method, then mix and teach each other.
*Good for:* several parallel methods of comparable difficulty — a survey section.
*Time:* 30–45 min. *Watch out for:* one weak group leaves a hole in everyone's
knowledge. Have the summary ready.

**Take a side**
Assigned positions, argued, then swapped.
*Good for:* genuine controversies. Assign the side; do not let them pick.
*Time:* 20–30 min. *Watch out for:* needs a question with two defensible answers.

**Play the role**
You are the CFO / the auditor / the regulator, looking at the same facts.
*Good for:* showing that the same numbers support different actions depending on
who you are.
*Time:* 20–30 min. *Watch out for:* give each role one sentence of what they care
about, or everyone argues the same way.

## 7. Recall it with nothing in front of you

The cheapest thing on this list and the most neglected. Trying to remember and
failing beats re-reading.

**Fill in the blank map**
The chapter's own diagram with the branches emptied. Fill it from memory.
*Good for:* closing a session. Costs nothing if a map already exists.
*Time:* 5–8 min. *Watch out for:* must be attempted before it is revealed.

**Two-minute brain dump**
Everything you remember about X, on paper, no notes. Then compare with a neighbour.
*Good for:* opening the *next* session, not closing this one.
*Time:* 4–6 min. *Watch out for:* the comparison step is what makes it work.

**Write the exam question**
Students write a question they think could be asked, and its marking scheme.
*Good for:* revision, and it tells you exactly what they think matters.
*Time:* 10–15 min. *Watch out for:* they will write what they can answer — that
is itself the diagnosis.

## 8. Use the AI, then catch it

Assume every student has an assistant. These teach them to direct it and to
catch it, which is the part that transfers.

**Write the prompt, then mark the answer**
Write a prompt, run it, then grade the output against a rubric. Hand in prompt,
output and critique.
*Good for:* making prompting a graded skill instead of a banned one. The prompt
is the artefact.
*Time:* 20–30 min. *Watch out for:* mark the critique, not the output. The best
submissions often contain the worst AI answers.

**Beat the machine**
Answer alone first, then ask the AI, then defend whichever you keep.
*Good for:* preserving the by-hand floor while acknowledging the tool exists.
*Time:* 20–30 min. *Watch out for:* the by-hand answer must be committed before
the tool is opened.

**Where did this come from?**
Given an AI output, work out what prompt produced it, and what the prompt left out.
*Good for:* teaching that the question shapes the answer.
*Time:* 10–15 min. *Watch out for:* keep the output short.

**Ask it to argue against you**
Get the strongest case against your own conclusion, then rebut it in writing.
*Good for:* one-sided arguments and confirmation bias.
*Time:* 15–20 min. *Watch out for:* students accept the counter-argument too
readily. The rebuttal is the assessed part.

## Choosing between them

**Match the format to the finding, not to novelty.**

| The audit found | Reach for |
|---|---|
| Questions on slides with no way to answer | Family 1 |
| A method taught without its weakness | Family 2 |
| Objectives an AI does in under a minute | Families 3 and 8 |
| Definitions stated before they are needed | Family 4 |
| Students who can compute but cannot explain | Family 5 |
| A silent room, or a very large one | Family 6 as a wrapper |
| Nothing that asks students to remember | Family 7 |
| An AI policy that is only a ban | Family 8 |

**Rules that hold whichever you pick**

- Everyone commits, then the split is shown, then the answer. Skipping the
  middle step loses most of the effect.
- Aim for one activity in the session with **no clean right answer**. Those are
  the ones students still remember.
- Never propose more than about four activities for one session. A lecture of
  activities is as tiring as a lecture of slides.
- Say the minutes out loud, and say what you would drop if it over-runs.
